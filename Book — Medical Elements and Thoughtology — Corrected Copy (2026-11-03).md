## 2.1 The shape-sorter, as a model

The body, in this model, is a shape-sorter: pieces — squirrel, circles, triangles, blocks — that can only go in one place [H]. At the molecular level, the model has two documented legs [D]. The first is receptor specificity: a virus binds a specific cell receptor, and the wrong receptor means no entry. The spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 binding ACE2 is the textbook case. The second is antibody–antigen fit: an antibody fits its target like a key in a lock, and a mismatched antibody does not neutralize. In both legs, shape is destiny at the binding step: the round peg must match the round hole to get in.

The model breaks as a literal wooden set, and the break is as instructive as the model [D]. Biology is not wood. Binding is flexible — induced fit changes the shape of both partners as they meet — and, decisively, one antibody can fit several cousin viruses imperfectly. The California serogroup of Chapter 3 is the proof: a single IgM result can react to La Crosse virus, Jamestown Canyon virus, and their cousins, and only a neutralizing-antibody comparison, with its 4-fold rule, can sometimes name the member — and sometimes cannot. A shape-sorter with exactly one hole per piece would predict no cross-reactivity. Cross-reactivity is documented. So shape holds as a specificity model and breaks as a literal set of wooden holes.

## 2.2 Disease is not the peg in the hole

His dictation says "what disease actually is is like a round virus can go in a round hole" [H]. Tested, that sentence splits into an entry condition and a definition, and only the first survives [LOGIC]. Shape complementarity is necessary for infection — the peg must match the hole to get in — but it is not sufficient for disease. Carriage without disease is documented across the record: Jamestown Canyon virus shows high seroprevalence with many asymptomatic infections, and pneumococcus colonizes the nasopharynx of healthy children without disease. A peg can sit in a hole quietly. What turns entry into disease is dose, site, immune state, and time — the same virus in the same hole produces different disease by how many arrived, where they landed, and how strong the defenses were that day. Shape decides entry. It does not decide sickness. That distinction is the six-track rule in miniature: the virus track (the peg) and the infection track (the peg in the hole) are not the disease track (what happens next), the illness track, or the sickness track.

## 2.3 The two-axis sorter — my labeled addition [LOGIC, mine, not his]

His Lincoln log set actually needs two sortings at once, and Chapter 3 proves it: **shape** (does the peg fit the hole — receptor, antibody fit) and **color** (which family or serogroup does the piece belong to). A piece can have the right shape for the wrong color's hole — that is cross-reactivity. A piece can have the right color and the wrong shape — that is a cousin virus that cannot infect you. Disease, in the full model, happens only when shape and color and size (Chapter 4) line up on the same piece at the same time. That is the shape-sorter upgraded to three axes, and it keeps his color spectrum without claiming viruses are physically colored.

## Simply put — Chapter 2

Your body has locks, and germs have keys. Most keys only fit one lock — but some keys are sloppy and jiggle open a cousin's lock too. Getting in the hole is only step one. Whether you get sick depends on how many got in, where they landed, and how strong your defenses were that day.

# Chapter 3 — Color: The Spectrum, and the California Serogroup Worked Example

**Source:** Lincoln Log theory P2 (November 1, 2026) and "Serotyping Information — California Serogroup, La Crosse, and Jamestown Canyon (2026-11-01)." Shiro: PENDING on every row below.

## 3.1 Color as a label, not a property

Circles in different colors, in his dictation, are viruses in "the spectrum of colors" [H]. Tested three ways, color splits cleanly. First, as a labeling system, color works exactly as he uses it [H→D]: in every textbook diagram, viruses are drawn in color — coronavirus red spikes, influenza blue — and the color means a family or a part. Second, as physics, color breaks [D]: viruses are 80–120 nm, far below visible light, and have no color of their own. Color in a microscope picture is false color added afterward. Third, as diversity, the spectrum is real [D]: viral diversity ranges from tiny parvoviruses (~20 nm) to giant poxviruses (~300 nm), from one genome segment to three (orthobunyaviruses) to many. So color holds as a sorting label and a diversity spectrum, and breaks as a physical property of the virus. The book keeps the label and drops the physics — and says so, in the open, rather than letting the reader mistake a diagram for a photograph.

## 3.2 The correction that has to come first

There is no separate "La Crosse serogroup" and no separate "Jamestown Canyon serogroup" in the record [D]. There is one serogroup — the California serogroup (CSG) — and La Crosse virus and Jamestown Canyon virus are two members inside it, alongside California encephalitis virus, snowshoe hare virus, trivittatus virus, and the rest. Keeping them separate as members is the honest version of the request that produced the serotyping file; collapsing them into three parallel serogroups would invent a taxonomy that does not exist.

What a "serogroup" means here is a group of viruses sorted by antibody cross-reactivity — the antibodies raised against one member react, to varying degrees, with the others [D]. This is not bacterial serotyping: there is no capsule chemistry here. The sorting key is serology plus genetics. The group sits in order Bunyavirales, family Peribunyaviridae, genus *Orthobunyavirus*; its members are trisegmented, negative-sense RNA viruses, about 90–100 nm, enveloped, with surface glycoprotein spikes. Its prototype is California encephalitis virus (CEV), first isolated from mosquitoes in California in 1943 [D].

Member count is carried as a variance, not smoothed over [D]: a 2019 review counts 18 recognized members based on serological and genetic evidence, while another source counts 17 — a counting and inclusion difference (Guaroa virus was briefly categorized in the group and later shown to be distinct). The U.S. surveillance short list — the six that matter for U.S. testing — is California encephalitis, Jamestown Canyon, Keystone, La Crosse, snowshoe hare, and trivittatus. At least seven members have been shown to cause neurological disease in humans, including La Crosse virus, the leading cause of pediatric arboviral encephalitis in the United States.

## 3.3 The two members, on their own values

**La Crosse virus (LACV) [D].** Member, not serogroup. Disease: La Crosse encephalitis, primarily in children, about 50–100 reported cases per year in the United States, the most pathogenic member of the group. Vector: the eastern tree-hole mosquito *Aedes triseriatus* (primary), with *Aedes canadensis* also reported; amplifying hosts chipmunks and squirrels. Diagnosis is serology first: virus is difficult to isolate from clinical samples, a preliminary diagnosis can be made by La Crosse-specific IgM in serum or cerebrospinal fluid, and because of potential cross-reactivity in the IgM assay to related viruses (for example Jamestown Canyon virus), a positive IgM result should be confirmed by neutralizing antibody testing at a state public health laboratory or CDC. Cross-reactivity profile: LACV is closely related to Jamestown Canyon virus and snowshoe hare virus, and in the mouse cross-reactivity study LACV, snowshoe hare virus, and Inkoo virus elicited the highest amount of cross-reactive neutralizing antibody.

**Jamestown Canyon virus (JCV) [D].** Member, not serogroup. First isolated in 1961 near Jamestown, Colorado. Age pattern is the mirror image of La Crosse: JCV preferentially causes neuroinvasive disease in adults, several cases per year in the United States and Canada, with reported cases rising substantially in the 2010s as diagnostic testing improved — considered a potentially emerging arboviral disease. Vector: multiple *Aedes* (and *Culiseta*) mosquitoes; primary amplifying host thought to be white-tailed deer; Wisconsin and Minnesota account for more than half of recognized U.S. cases. Case counts, three figures with three scopes, all carried: 31 disease cases in the United States from 2000 to 2013 (older CDC summary); 281 confirmed infections in the United States from 2011 to 2022 across 23 states; and a literature review from 1982 to 2022 finding 416 cases, with a 2.4% fatality rate among hospitalized encephalitis cases. Diagnosis is serology first with a JCV-specific trap: a positive JCV or California serogroup IgM result should be confirmed by neutralizing antibody testing, it is not known how long IgM can be detected after infection (so a positive IgM may reflect past infection), and the most accurate recent-infection call comes from acute and convalescent sera showing at least a 4-fold rise in neutralizing antibody titers — or JCV-specific neutralizing antibodies at least 4-fold higher than La Crosse-specific titers in the same or a later specimen. Even that gold standard has a limit, kept in the file: a 4-fold difference is not always observed, and in one recent seroprevalence study two specimens had indistinguishable neutralizing titers between Jamestown Canyon and La Crosse viruses. A single IgM result, without neutralizing-antibody confirmation, does not name the member.

## 3.4 What "serotyping" does and does not mean here

It groups these viruses by shared antibody reactions (serogroup), then tries to name the single member by the highest neutralizing titer, using the 4-fold rule [D]. It does not give each virus a capsule type the way pneumococcus gets serotypes 1, 3, and 23F. There is no capsule, and no 100-type list. The California serogroup's "types" are the 17–18 member viruses themselves. That asymmetry — letter groups for meningococcus, numbered serotypes for pneumococcus, serogroup members for California viruses — is why the three systems do not translate into each other, a point the book returns to in Chapter 7 and Appendix D.

# Chapter 4 — Size: Small and Large, and the Two Different Shortages

**Source:** "Theory — Virus Size Imbalance, Missing Viruses, and Immunoglobulin Therapy (2026-11-01)," Douglas's dictation ~1:32 PM CST, November 1, 2026. Shiro: PENDING. Naming: American term first, Latin second where applicable (see Chapter 1).

Two readings are flagged and carried, not collapsed: "aren't conjoined contingent" is read as "are not necessarily joined together / do not necessarily occur together"; "short a certain viruses" carries three readings (below), not one.

## 4.1 Imbalance within the size of viruses

Viruses span roughly 20 nm (parvoviruses) to 300 nm (poxviruses), with the ~100 nm band crowded by coronaviruses, orthobunyaviruses, arenaviruses, retroviruses, and influenza [D]. Any one sample is therefore skewed — it is not an even spread across sizes. In the virome literature, that skew has a name and a pattern: dysbiosis in viral communities — changes in temperate phage activity, reductions in viral diversity, or expansion of pathogenic viruses — is associated with mucosal inflammation, microbial dysbiosis, and systemic disease [D]. In inflammatory bowel disease, studies show an increase in Caudovirales (tailed double-stranded DNA bacteriophages) alongside a decrease in general viral diversity and richness [D]. Imbalance, in the sense of a skewed community, is documented. But "imbalance within the size" is not itself a standard measurement [D]: studies measure abundance, diversity, and richness by kind, not by a size-balance score. Size imbalance as a cause is therefore [GAP]; size skew as a description of a skewed virome HOLDS. The chapter keeps that distinction, because a description that is mistaken for a cause will send every later test looking in the wrong place.

## 4.2 Small and large viruses are usually not conjoined

Most infections are single-virus events; a small virus does not require a large partner to infect, and vice versa [D]. Co-infection happens; contingency does not. California serogroup viruses co-circulate in the same woods and mosquitoes, and cross-react on tests, without being joined particles: together in a place is not together in a body [D]. The exception sharpens the rule rather than breaking it: satellite viruses and virophages genuinely are contingent on a helper virus [D] — the exception proves the rule is "often," not "always." Douglas's "aren't conjoined contingent" therefore HOLDS as a general pattern, with satellite viruses filed as the named exception.

## 4.3 Short a certain virus — three shortages, not one

**(a) Short of antibodies to a virus [D].** Seronegative because never exposed. Common, normal, not a shortage in the disease sense. The test is a serology panel: are antibodies to virus X present or absent?

**(b) Short of a virome member [D, as association].** Reduced diversity, as in the inflammatory bowel disease pattern above. Documented as association, not yet as a proven missing-piece cause for a named person. The test is virome sequencing: is virus X itself present or absent in the sample?

**(c) Short of a beneficial virus [H→D].** The phage version: loss of phages that kept a bacterium in check, letting that bacterium expand. Documented in the gut literature as a mechanism; per-virus proof is still developing.

The causal claim — "a missing virus makes this person sick" — is [GAP] until a specific virus, a specific person-pattern, and a specific test are named. A person can fail test (a) and test (b) independently, and the book never lets one failure stand in for the other.

## 4.4 Immunoglobulin therapy — the direction matters

Spikes are receptor-binding proteins, and "use" is correct in the functional sense: the spike is the tool the virus uses to attach and enter [D]. ## 6.2 Viruses do not eat; the biome does

Viruses do not eat, nor do they possess a metabolism to convert food into energy; unlike bacteria, fungi, or animals, they lack the cellular machinery required to intake nutrients, break down molecular bonds, or utilize ATP [D]. What viruses do is convert one population into another: phage lysis turns living bacteria into debris, which the body's clearance systems then remove [D]. Bacteriophages regulate gut bacterial communities through direct predation and genetic modulation, and by preying on harmful bacteria, phages can improve the gut microbiome [D]. That is clearing of a population, by killing its members — not eating.

## 6.3 The relay, step by step — my labeled addition [LOGIC, mine, not his]

**(1) Finger grabs cell.** Spike meets receptor (Chapter 5). **(2) Virus replicates; the cell bursts (phage) or is killed by immunity (eukaryotic virus).** **(3) Debris enters mucus, blood, or gut lumen.** **(4) Phagocytes, liver, kidney, cilia, and bowel clear it.** Douglas's "clearing mechanism" holds beautifully as this four-step relay. ## Simply put — Chapter 6

Your body clears things with mucus, antibodies, and cleanup cells. Some viruses clear bacteria by killing them. Bacteria and your gut bugs do eat — they really do chew through waste. A virus can kill a bacterium, and then your cleanup crew hauls away what's left. That's a relay race, not one eater.

## 7.3 Overburden equals swelling — and swelling equals neither encephalitis nor elephantiasis

**His dictation, verbatim [H] (1:39 PM):**

Each equation is tested, and each breaks as an equation while holding as a consequence or a shared column.

**Overburden → swelling [H→D].** A heavy microbial or inflammatory load produces edema — fluid leaking from vessels, cells swelling, tissue enlarging. But swelling also occurs without a microbial overburden (allergy, trauma, venous or lymphatic blockage, kidney or heart failure), and a high viral or bacterial load can exist with little visible swelling (bloodstream infection, early pneumonia, latent carriage) [D]. Overburden equals swelling BREAKS as an equation; overburden frequently causes swelling HOLDS.

**Swelling = encephalitis [BREAKS as equation].** Encephalitis is an inflammation (irritation and swelling) of the brain, usually caused by infections, and the brain tissue swells (cerebral edema), which may cause destruction of nerve cells [D]. A swollen ankle, lung, or limb is not encephalitis. Site plus inflammation plus brain dysfunction are all required.

**Swelling = elephantiasis [BREAKS as equation; "elephantitis" is his word].** Elephantiasis, also known as lymphatic filariasis, is a chronic disease that primarily affects the lymphatic system, characterized by abnormal swelling and enlargement of body parts, most commonly the limbs and genitalia [D]. It is not acute inflammatory swelling, and it is not brain swelling. Encephalitis and elephantiasis share exactly one element — swelling — and differ in site (brain versus limb/genital), time course (acute days versus chronic years), mechanism (inflammatory edema versus lymphatic obstruction and fibrosis), and cause (usually virus versus parasitic worm) [D]. Sharing an element is a cross-reference, not an identity. **Swelling is a shared showing column, not an identity between encephalitis and elephantiasis.** That sentence is the verdict of this section, and the appendix table (Table F) is built to prove it column by column.

## 7.4 Meningococcal pneumonia and pneumococcal pneumonia: a cross-reference, not the same disease

Both organisms cause both syndromes [D]: about 80% of adult bacterial meningitis cases are now caused by *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Neisseria meningitidis*, and pneumococcus is the most common cause of community-acquired pneumonia and of bacterial meningitis. Meningococcus causes meningitis and septicemia primarily, with pneumonia as a less common presentation — so "meningococcal pneumonia" is real but rarer than pneumococcal pneumonia, and the two are not symmetric in frequency [D]. Both are encapsulated diplococci [D]: *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Neisseria meningitidis* are given as examples of encapsulated diplococci, and all three classic pathogens (with *Haemophilus influenzae*) possess a capsule that protects them from phagocytosis. Capsule logic is the genuine cross-reference — the same defense, different capsule chemistry: pneumococcal numbered serotypes versus meningococcal letter serogroups, which do not translate into each other (per the October 28–31 files). Showing is not causation (carriage shows without disease), and showing both is not the same disease. The cross-reference HOLDS at the level of "same sample, same syndrome, same capsule defense" and BREAKS at "same disease." **Meningococcal and pneumococcal pneumonia are a cross-reference, not the same disease.**

## 10.1 Viruses are not fungal material

Viruses are nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) in a protein capsid, sometimes in a lipid envelope taken from a host cell [D]. They are not made of fungal cells — fungi are eukaryotic cells with chitin walls, nuclei, and metabolism. A virus that infects a fungus is still virus material, not fungus material. Viruses infect every domain — bacteria (bacteriophages), archaea, fungi (mycoviruses), plants, and animals [D]. A fungal-only origin would predict no bacterial viruses, and bacterial viruses are the most abundant viruses on Earth. So "viruses are fungal material only, not bacterial material" BREAKS as material identity, and HOLDS only as co-presence in lung and bowel samples, where sputum and stool carry bacteria, fungi, and viruses together (Chapters 8 and 9). Co-presence in a sample is real; shared material is not.

## 10.2 Bacteria do have viruses; fungi do not always have them

**Viruses hang out with bacteria [H] — HOLDS, strongly.** Bacteriophages are viruses that infect bacteria, abundant in the human gut [D]; human innards are teeming with viruses that infect bacteria [D]. Many bacteria carry phage DNA in their own genomes (lysogeny), and a gut study found active polylysogeny in 58% of studied gut lysogens [D]. Diphtheria toxin, cholera toxin, and Shiga toxin genes are phage-carried — the strongest foothold from his October 29 filing [D]. A bacterium's most famous weapon can literally be a virus's gene.

**Bacteria alone do not have viruses with them [H] — BREAKS as a general claim.** The gut alone holds billions to trillions of phages, and each of Douglas's three diseases has its own documented phage: several pneumococcal phages have been isolated, including Cp-1 infecting *Streptococcus pneumoniae* [D]; a mycobacteriophage is a member of a group of bacteriophages known to have mycobacteria as host bacterial species, with more than 4,200 mycobacteriophage species isolated, and mycobacteriophage D29 used to detect viable *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* in sputum [D]; pneumococcus causes pneumococcal meningitis, and meningococcal and other bacterial meningitis pathogens likewise carry prophages and phage elements in their genomes as a general pattern (strain-specific proof is per-strain, [GAP] for any single unnamed strain). What can be true is narrower: a particular bacterial strain, in a particular sample, may show no detectable phage. That is absence of detection, not absence of phages from bacteria as a class [LOGIC].

**Fungus always does have viruses with it [H] — BREAKS as "always"; HOLDS as "common, sometimes very common, varying by fungus."** Mycoviruses are widespread and purportedly common throughout the fungal kingdom, and fungal viruses have been reported from all major fungal taxonomic groups [D]. But a large survey found 21.6% of 333 isolates positive for RNA mycoviruses in early-diverging fungi [D], other screens vary wildly (*Aspergillus fumigatus* 6.6%, *Beauveria bassiana* 54.8%, some fungi up to 80–97% in particular screens), and it has been estimated that 30–80% of fungal species are infected [D] — a wide, common range, not 100%. In a selected sample — a diseased lung, a moldy culture, a fungus already chosen because it is sick — mycovirus rates run high, and mixed infections are common. Selection makes "often" look like "always" [H→D].

## 10.3 The rewritten connection

Bacteria-without-viruses versus fungus-always-with-viruses cannot be the missing connection, because both halves break in the tests above [LOGIC]. A connection that does survive testing is subtler, and it is the chapter's heading in rewritten form: **bacteria and fungi both carry viruses, but carry them differently** — bacteria carry phages that often kill them or arm them with toxin genes; fungi carry mycoviruses that usually persist latently and change the fungus's virulence (hypovirulence) rather than killing it [D]. That asymmetry — lethal/arming versus latent/modulating — is a real, testable difference, and it fits his bowel-and-lung diseases better than a fungal-only rule. The bowel/lung link is the observation behind it: gut and lung samples mix all three kingdoms in one sputum or stool sample, so a researcher reading those samples would keep seeing virus–fungus–bacterium trios together. The trio is the observation; the fungal-only rule is one interpretation of it, and the data favor the trio interpretation.

His learning path, as a path, HOLDS [H]: meningitis and pneumonia are where bacterial, fungal, and viral disease meet in one syndrome; pneumococcus and tuberculosis each have their own documented phages; bowel and lungs are where mixed-kingdom samples are actually read. Whether the conclusion (fungal-only) follows from that path is what this chapter tests, and it does not — but the path itself, as how he learned to see the trio, is filed as his, intact.

## 10.4 The test-percentage rule, in conditional form

His 1:38 PM dictation defends "always" with a statistical argument, and the argument is tested here the same way. A percentage does license a probability: if 90 of 100 tested fungi carry a virus, the next tested fungus has roughly a 90% prior chance [D, base-rate logic]. That instinct is sound. But the percentage licenses a probability for the same kind of case that was tested [D]: a rate from sick, cultured, or clinically selected fungi does not transfer to all fungi everywhere. This is selection bias, the oldest trap in test-percentage reasoning. If his tested set runs near 100% while the general survey runs at 21.6%, his set is a selected set — diseased, cultured, or otherwise filtered — and the file must say which, or the percentage misleads [LOGIC].

The conditional save, preserved beside his original "always," is the version this book carries: **"In the kind of diseased fungus I test, assume a virus until a test says otherwise, and test when it matters."** That version is testable, falsifiable, and honest. The book's standing rule for every such assumption, my labeled addition [LOGIC, mine, not his], is: **write the percentage on the assumption, every time** — "In [this kind of fungus, tested this way], [X]% carried a virus; in untested cases of the same kind, I assume a virus at that percentage, labeled as an assumption, not a finding." A bet is not a test result, and "assumed present, untested" is a different tier from "detected." His own standing rule says [GAP] stays [GAP] where the record is empty at 51% — and "always" claims 100%.

## 10.5 Clearing, byproduct, and what illness is

**When you clear disease, the viruses and the fungus are no longer there [H] — HOLDS for the acute pair, BREAKS universal.** For an acute pathogenic fungus or virus, recovery tracks falling load and, often, clearance below detection [D]. But commensal fungi stay: *Candida albicans*, often associated with infections, was detected in 63.6% of healthy participants, and as a gut commensal it is capable of adapting to the intestinal environment without causing harm under normal circumstances [D]. Commensal viruses stay: anelloviruses are acquired in infancy and maintain a lifelong, dynamic presence, with prevalence in healthy adults approaching 100% [D]. Endogenous viruses never leave, because they are in the genome [D]. Healthy people carry fungi and viruses after recovery — and before sickness.

**Fungus is always a byproduct of the biome [H] — HOLDS partial.** Fungi are commensal members of the host, prevalent in various body sites such as the gut, lungs, and skin [D]: the mycobiome is a part of the biome, not merely a product of it. Fungi make byproducts (metabolites, cell-wall components, toxins in some species) that affect bacteria, host immunity, and other fungi [D]. Member and producer are both true; "byproduct only" drops the member half. A fungus that colonizes from the environment (dietary *Saccharomyces*, inhaled spores) is not a byproduct of your biome at all — it is an input to it [LOGIC].

**When you're sick there will be an overburden, and the biome changes because of the sickness of the bugs and us both [H] — HOLDS as a two-way loop in biome-linked sickness.** Dysbiosis — reduced diversity, expansion of one group — is documented in sickness [D]. The loop is the disease process: host inflammation changes the microbial environment (oxygen, mucus, bile, antibiotics), which changes the microbes, which changes host inflammation further [H→D]. Neither side is the sole cause. Not every sickness shows a biome overburden first (trauma, genetic disease, single-pathogen infections with a normal background biome are counterexamples) [D], so the loop is a common pattern, not a universal gate.

**Biome byproduct is what illness actually is [H, his definition] — HOLDS as a definition for biome-linked illness, with the three-track mapping shown.** If illness is defined, in his system, as the changed biome's output — altered metabolites, shed organisms, inflammatory signals — then the definition is coherent and testable: measure the byproduct, name the illness [H→D]. Against his six tracks, it does not collapse them if disease stays as the named condition and sickness stays as the felt state, with illness as the biome's changed output between them. The book shows that three-part mapping explicitly, or the definition would read as a collapse. As a universal definition of all illness it BREAKS: a broken bone's illness, a genetic illness, a purely toxic illness need no biome byproduct to be illness [D].

## Simply put — Chapter 10

Viruses aren't made of fungus. They're their own thing, and they can infect fungi, bacteria, animals, and plants alike. Bacteria very often do have viruses with them — that's what a phage is. Fungi often carry viruses — sometimes most of them in a batch do. But not always. One survey found only about 1 in 5 early fungi carrying one. The missing connection isn't "only fungi have viruses." It's that bacteria and fungi relate to their viruses differently — bacteria get killed or armed by theirs, fungi mostly live with theirs quietly. And don't write "always" where you mean "in the sick fungi I actually test, almost always." The second sentence is strong and true. The first overreaches and breaks on the numbers. When you beat a bad infection, that bad fungus and that bad virus do go away. But the good fungi in your gut and the quiet viruses you've carried since infancy stay — they were never the disease.

## 11.2 The method, step by step

**Step 1: Form every idea as a theory or postulate, and save it [H, standing rule].** Dictation is not conversation to be lost; it is material to be filed, verbatim, with its date and its readings flagged. A theory that is not saved cannot be tested, and a theory that is paraphrased into safety cannot be tested honestly either. That is why every chapter in this book opens with Douglas's dictation verbatim, garble and double negatives included, before any test begins.

**Step 2: Split compound dictations, so no sub-idea is lost [H, standing rule].** One dictation that holds more than one idea becomes one postulate per idea, each tested on its own. The Lincoln Log dictation became seven postulates; the sputum and stool dictation became eight; the fungus dictation became six, and its defense became seven more. Collapsing would have hidden the breaks: in the fungus case, the material-identity claim breaks while the trio-observation claim holds, and only splitting shows both.

**Step 3: Test each postulate at least three different ways before the first answer, when possible [H, standing rule].** Three ways means three independent legs — a documented mechanism, a documented count or case, and a documented counterexample or limit — not three restatements of the same leg. The swelling chapter is the model: overburden-to-swelling is tested by consequence, by swelling-without-overburden, and by overburden-without-swelling. A postulate that survives three legs is stronger than one that survives one, and a postulate that breaks on the third leg is more instructive than one that was never pushed that far.

**Step 4: Never let the method limit what is given [H, standing rule].** The discipline is in the testing, not in holding back. Recommendations are made even when uncertain, with confidence labels kept honest — verified versus untested idea — rather than withheld for fear of being wrong. A close-but-wrong suggestion helps brainstorming toward the right answer. The method's rigor is a reason to give more, tested, not less, untested.

**Step 5: When he agrees with a standard or documented fact, postulate it as a theorem paralleling the known information [H, standing rule].** His agreement, stated formally alongside the record, is not absorbed into the record and is not left as mere assent. It is filed as his theorem, with the documented fact beside it. Conversely, when his dictation and the record disagree, the combined-statement method applies (Step 6).

**Step 6: The combined-statement method [H, his explicit endorsement, October 19, 2026].** Take his statement, test it against the record, and finish the thought in a way that is not illogical. His thoughts plus the book's thoughts produce the correction and statement of fact that fits. In his words, the method "finishes my thoughts in a way that is not illogical," and "that's what I'm looking for." Every rewrite in this book — the conditional fungus assumption, colonization for chunk, trajectory for overburden, relay for consumption — is a combined statement, and each is labeled as such, with the original beside it.

**Step 7: The classroom method [H, standing since October 31, 2026].** His questions and theories may be intentionally wrong, posed deliberately so the conversation can test them and show what holds. He is teaching: building a textbook and teaching classroom material through these postulations. A "breaks" verdict is not a failed theory and not a correction of him; it is the class working. Test straight, keep verdicts honest, and let the showing be the point — the conversation itself is what he calls the miracle of what AI can show him.

**Step 8: The 51% bar [H, standing since October 18, 2026].** For calculations with medicine and association claims, 51% — documented support at the majority level — counts as implied potential yes over no. What an older, higher bar excluded as "documented but debated" now enters, with the honest caveat filed beside it. [GAP] stays where the record is empty at 51%. This bar is why this book's verdicts say HOLDS partial as often as they say HOLDS: a majority-supported claim with a filed caveat is a holding, not a hedge.

**Step 9: The six tracks, the dual register, old-with-new, and one extra thing [H, standing rules].** Think vaccines, viruses, diseases, illnesses, sicknesses, and infections separately every time; never collapse the six. Carry the 13th-grade treatment and the 6th-to-8th-grade "Simply put" telling side by side in every chapter; simple words are respectful, simplified ideas are not. Leave the old alongside the new first; removal comes later, only if needed, unless something is completely obviously wrong in a way that can never be rewritten. And every deliverable gets one extra addition of the builder's own judgment, clearly labeled as mine — the piece judged needed for completeness that was not asked for, with provenance on everything, so the final answer knows where it comes from: his dictation, the record, or the builder's addition.

**Step 10: Thought experiments that go back in time [H, October 17, 2026, §179].** In his own method statement, verbatim: "My thought experiments go back in time and utilize medical experimentation that was already available for me to study. And I tried to fill in these gaps by thinking about the conditions that weren't thought about and the controls that weren't thought about. And I reassessed a lot of old medicine that way. And that's why my thought process works the way it does." Thought experiments are a recognized instrument, and retrospective re-analysis is real medicine [D]. The seam, stated honestly, is that the retrospective experiment proposes; it does not replace the missing control. Hypothesis-generation is strong; verdict needs the bench [D]. Manuscript ideas and general theories get full honest pushback with tier labels on every claim [H, standing rule]. | Pneumococcal pneumonia | *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (pneumococcus) | Yes — pneumococcal phages documented | Fungi co-occur in sputum; fungal pneumonia is a separate disease | Respiratory viruses co-occur and often precede pneumococcal disease (influenza-first pattern) | PENDING |
| Tuberculosis | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* | Yes — mycobacteriophages, 4,200+ species isolated; D29 detects viable bacilli in sputum | Fungi co-occur in sputum and lung samples; fungal lung disease is separate | Respiratory and gut viruses co-occur in the same patient samples | PENDING |

The table's lesson, in one line: bacteria and fungi both carry viruses, differently — and the trio in a sample is an observation, not a fungal-only law.

## Table E — Immunoglobulin versus virus-gap test pair (see Chapter 4)

| Gap | Test | What a positive / negative result means | Therapy the test points to | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody gap (short of antibodies to virus X) | Serology panel for virus X | Absent antibodies = never exposed, or unable to make antibodies | Immunoglobulin replacement is the documented replacement for antibody deficiency (clinician's decision; no recommendation here) | PENDING |
| Virus gap (short of virus X itself) | Virome sequencing of the sample | Absent virus = not present in that sample at detection limit; not proof of absence everywhere | No proven "virus replacement" therapy in the current record; [GAP] | PENDING |

A person can fail either test independently. Never let one failure stand in for the other.

## Table F — Swelling as a column (see Chapter 7)

| Disease (American, Latin second where applicable) | Site of swelling | Time course | Mechanism of swelling | Usual cause | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Encephalitis (Latin: *encephalitis*) | Brain | Acute, days | Inflammatory edema inside a rigid skull; neuronal dysfunction and destruction | Usually virus (also bacteria, fungi, immune causes) | PENDING |
| Elephantiasis (Latin: *elephantiasis*; Douglas's word: "elephantitis") | Limbs, genitalia | Chronic, years | Lymphatic obstruction and fibrosis; skin thickening and hardening | Most often filarial parasitic worms (*Wuchereria bancrofti*, *Brugia* spp.) | PENDING |
| Pneumococcal pneumonia (Latin: *pneumonia*) | Lung | Acute, days | Inflammatory consolidation and edema in lung tissue | *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (pneumococcus) | PENDING |
| Meningococcal disease (Latin: *meningitis* for the meningeal form) | Meninges, blood | Acute, hours to days | Inflammation, edema, endotoxin-mediated shock | *Neisseria meningitidis* (meningococcus) | PENDING |
| Swollen limb, ankle, or joint (general showing) | Any tissue | Any | Edema from allergy, trauma, venous or lymphatic blockage, heart or kidney failure — many causes | Not a single disease | PENDING |

The table's lesson, in one line: swelling is a shared showing column. Every other column differs. An equation built on one matching column — overburden = swelling = encephalitis = elephantiasis — feels right and breaks on every other column. Keep swelling as a column, and the cross-reference holds.
